DVI-A Interface Maximum Analog Bandwidth

The DVI-A (Digital Visual Interface – Analog) interface is designed exclusively to transmit analog video signals from a host computer to an analog display device, such as a legacy CRT monitor or an analog-input LCD. This article explains the maximum analog bandwidth supported by DVI-A, detailing its technical frequency limits, how it translates into display resolutions and refresh rates, and how it compares to digital DVI specifications.

Maximum Analog Bandwidth Specification

The DVI-A interface supports a maximum analog bandwidth of 400 MHz (-3 dB).

Unlike the digital pins in DVI-D or DVI-I—which are bound by strict digital clock frequencies and Transition Minimized Differential Signaling (TMDS) limits—the analog portion of DVI operates identically to high-end analog RGB (VGA) connections. The 400 MHz limit is determined primarily by the hardware RAMDAC (Random-Access Memory Digital-to-Analog Converter) located on the graphics card and the electrical signaling characteristics of the DVI-A connector pins.

Resolutions and Refresh Rates Supported

With a 400 MHz bandwidth, DVI-A can handle resolutions and refresh rates that exceed the requirements of standard 1080p video. Because analog signals do not require TMDS data framing, the bandwidth directly limits the maximum pixel clock frequency:

  • 1920 × 1200 (WUXGA) at 60 Hz: Requires an approximate pixel clock of 154 MHz, well within the 400 MHz ceiling.
  • 2048 × 1536 (QXGA) at 60 Hz: Requires a pixel clock of approximately 209 MHz.
  • 2048 × 1536 (QXGA) at 85 Hz: Requires a pixel clock of roughly 388 MHz, reaching the upper practical limit of the 400 MHz analog specification.

Pinout and Signal Handling

DVI-A routes the analog signal using dedicated pins grouped around the flat ground blade on the connector:

  • C1: Analog Red
  • C2: Analog Green
  • C3: Analog Blue
  • C4: Analog Horizontal Sync
  • C5: Analog Ground (Return for RGB signals)
  • Pin 8: Analog Vertical Sync

Because DVI-A does not populate or connect the digital TMDS data channels, it functions as a native analog connector. Passive adapters can convert DVI-A to a standard 15-pin VGA (DE-15) connector without signal conversion, retaining the full 400 MHz analog potential provided the display cable and monitor can resolve the signal without attenuation.

Comparison to Digital DVI Limits

  • DVI-A (Analog): Operates up to 400 MHz analog bandwidth.
  • DVI-D Single-Link (Digital): Operates up to a maximum pixel clock of 165 MHz (yielding up to 3.96 Gbps of video data), which limits standard digital displays to 1920 × 1200 at 60 Hz.
  • DVI-D Dual-Link (Digital): Employs two TMDS transmitters to bypass the 165 MHz cap, supporting resolutions such as 2560 × 1600 at 60 Hz.

While high-frequency analog video provides high resolutions over DVI-A, signal quality degrades over long cable runs due to analog crosstalk, impedance mismatches, and cable capacitance, making digital DVI connections preferable for modern high-resolution applications.
