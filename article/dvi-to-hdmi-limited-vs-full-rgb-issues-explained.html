DVI to HDMI Limited vs Full RGB Issues Explained

Converting a DVI connection to HDMI often results in washed-out colors or crushed shadow details due to dynamic range mismatches between Full and Limited RGB color spaces. This issue stems from the differing design origins of the two display standards, where DVI was engineered exclusively for computers using a 0–255 color range, while HDMI inherited television broadcast standards that default to a restricted 16–235 range. When adapting between these two interfaces, the source device and the display frequently miscommunicate their intended color levels during the digital handshake.

Full RGB vs. Limited RGB

RGB dynamic range defines how many luminance steps are used to display color:

  • Full RGB (0–255): The standard color space for personal computers and monitors. Black is defined at value 0, and reference white is defined at value 255, allowing for the maximum range of contrast and detail.
  • Limited RGB (16–235): The legacy standard developed for broadcast television and consumer video equipment. Black is anchored at 16, and white is anchored at 235. Values below 16 (toe room) and above 235 (headroom) are reserved for signal margins to prevent clipping in video mastering.

The Cause of the Conversion Issue

Pure DVI carries only VESA computer display signaling, which implicitly operates in Full RGB. It lacks the protocol support for Consumer Electronics Association (CEA-861) InfoFrames, the metadata packets that modern HDMI connections use to negotiate advanced color formats, audio, and dynamic range settings.

When a passive adapter or cable connects a DVI port to an HDMI port, two distinct communication errors typically occur:

  1. Display Misidentification: Because the display receives the signal via an HDMI port, it often assumes the input source is a consumer video device, such as a set-top box or Blu-ray player. Consequently, the display automatically switches its internal decoder to Limited RGB (16–235).
  2. EDID and Handshake Confusion: The display sends its Extended Display Identification Data (EDID) back to the graphics card. If the EDID lists standard television broadcast resolutions (such as 1080p or 720p), the graphics driver may misinterpret the connection as a TV instead of a PC monitor. Depending on the driver, the GPU might either send a Limited RGB signal to accommodate the display or maintain a Full RGB signal while the display enforces Limited RGB.

The Resulting Visual Defects

When the source and the display do not match, image quality degrades in one of two ways:

  • Source sends Full RGB (0–255), Display expects Limited RGB (16–235): The display maps value 16 as total black and value 235 as maximum white. Any detail in the incoming signal between 0 and 16 is clipped into solid black ("crushed blacks"), and details between 235 and 255 clip into solid white. The image appears overly contrasted with lost shadow and highlight details.
  • Source sends Limited RGB (16–235), Display expects Full RGB (0–255): The display expects black to be at 0, but the lowest signal it receives from the source is 16. As a result, pure blacks appear dark gray, whites appear dim, and the entire image looks hazy and washed out.

How to Resolve the Mismatch

Correcting this issue requires manually synchronizing the dynamic range between the graphics card and the receiving display.

On the display side, locate settings labeled "HDMI Black Level," "RGB Dynamic Range," or "Black Level." Setting this option to "High," "Full," or "Normal" configures the display for the 0–255 range, while "Low" or "Limited" locks it to 16–235. Alternatively, renaming the active HDMI input label to "PC" on many modern televisions automatically forces the port into Full RGB mode.

On the computer side, open the GPU management utility (NVIDIA Control Panel, AMD Radeon Software, or Intel Graphics Command Center). Under the display or color settings, find the "Dynamic Range" or "Pixel Format" setting and change it from Limited to Full (0–255), ensuring it matches the mode selected on the display.
