How the Apple Display Connector Sent DVI, USB, and Power

Introduced by Apple in 2000, the Apple Display Connector (ADC) eliminated cable clutter by transmitting digital video, USB data, and monitor power over a single physical cable. Designed to connect Power Mac systems to Apple Studio Displays and Cinema Displays, the connector combined standard digital display wiring with dedicated USB conductors and high-voltage DC power. By routing extra power through a modified motherboard slot directly to the graphics card, Apple integrated three distinct interfaces into a unified 35-pin connector.

The 35-Pin Architecture

The ADC port was mechanically based on a modified DVI-I connector, expanded to accommodate 35 pins. Standard DVI connectors feature 24 digital pins along with a cross-shaped analog pin cluster. Apple altered this layout to pack three separate wiring paths into a single reinforced shielding:

  • Digital and Analog Video Lines: ADC included standard Transition-Minimized Differential Signaling (TMDS) pairs for single-link DVI, providing digital resolutions up to 1920x1200. It also included analog RGB lines and Display Data Channel (DDC) pins to ensure backward compatibility with Apple's CRT displays.
  • USB Signal Lines: Dedicated pins were reserved for standard USB 1.1 data lines (Data+ and Data-).
  • Power and Ground Rails: Heavy-gauge pins were dedicated to carrying high-current DC power and common ground.

Delivering Power: The Extended AGP Slot

Supplying enough electricity to power an external 22-inch LCD monitor—or a CRT display—required more wattage than standard graphics buses could supply. Apple solved this by altering the graphics hardware inside the Mac:

  1. Extended Motherboard Slots: Apple modified the AGP (Accelerated Graphics Port) 4X slot on the Power Mac G4 motherboard, adding an extra power-delivery connector segment adjacent to the slot.
  2. Direct PSU Delivery: A dedicated line from the main computer power supply fed 25 volts of direct current (DC) directly to this extended slot.
  3. Card-Level Routing: ADC-equipped graphics cards received this 25V feed (delivering up to 100 watts) directly through the motherboard trace extensions, routing the power directly out through the ADC port's power pins without passing it through standard PCI/AGP power regulation circuits.

Integrating USB Hub Functionality

Apple monitors of that era featured built-in USB hubs to let users connect keyboards, mice, and peripherals directly to the display. Rather than requiring a separate USB uplink cable from the display to the tower, the ADC cable carried the USB D+ and D- signals directly from the computer’s internal USB controller.

The graphics card acted as an internal pass-through or sat on a bus capable of routing these USB lanes out through the ADC port. Once the signal reached the monitor, the onboard controller used the 25V supply (stepped down to 5V internally) to power the built-in USB hub.

Signal Isolation and Shielding

Running a 25V DC power line carrying up to 4 amps immediately adjacent to high-speed TMDS video lines and sensitive USB data channels introduced significant risk of electromagnetic interference (EMI). Apple addressed this with heavy internal shielding inside the molded cable and port assembly. Ground pins were interleaved between signal groups to prevent crosstalk, ensuring that high-current power delivery did not corrupt the video clock or digital data packets.
