Why HDMI and DVI Streams Are Electrically Identical

HDMI and single-link DVI video signals are electrically identical because they share the exact same underlying digital transmission protocol, physical signaling architecture, and voltage parameters. Designed with backward compatibility in mind, HDMI adopted DVI's video transport layer without altering the fundamental electrical characteristics of the video data. Because the digital video payload is transported using the same physical transmission methods, a standard passive adapter or cable can route video between the two formats without any active digital-to-analog or digital-to-digital signal conversion.

At the core of both HDMI and single-link DVI is Transition-Minimized Differential Signaling (TMDS). Developed by Silicon Image, TMDS is the signaling protocol used to transmit high-speed serial data over copper twisted pairs. In a standard single-link setup, both interfaces utilize four distinct differential pairs: three data lanes dedicated to the primary color channels (Red, Green, and Blue, or components) and one dedicated clock lane that synchronizes the data transmission.

The electrical specifications of these TMDS lines are identical in both standards. Each differential pair operates with a current-mode logic (CML) interface, terminated to a 3.3-volt supply. The signal transitions use an 8b/10b encoding scheme, which minimizes electromagnetic interference and ensures a DC-balanced signal across the cable. Because the electrical voltages, impedance matching (typically 100 ohms differential), and signaling rates for 8-bit RGB video match entirely, the receiving hardware cannot distinguish the raw video stream of an HDMI source from that of a single-link DVI source at the physical layer.

This deep parity exists because the founders of the HDMI Working Group specifically designed HDMI as a consumer-friendly evolution of the DVI 1.0 specification. Rather than reinventing the digital video transport layer, the engineers maintained the TMDS video specification while shrinking the connector size and adding modern multimedia capabilities.

The primary differences between the two formats occur in the metadata and auxiliary data, not the baseline electrical video stream. HDMI utilizes the "blanking intervals"—the brief pauses between drawing lines and frames of video—to transmit digital audio packets, Consumer Electronics Control (CEC) data, and colorimetry metadata via "Data Islands." When an HDMI source communicates with a native DVI display, it detects the display's capabilities via the DDC (Display Data Channel) and enters a legacy DVI-compatible mode. In this mode, the HDMI transmitter ceases broadcasting auxiliary data islands and transmits pure TMDS video, maintaining total electrical and functional interoperability with the DVI receiver.
