What Was Apple ADC and How Did It Relate to DVI?

In the early 2000s, Apple introduced the Apple Display Connector (ADC), a proprietary interface designed to streamline desktop computing by merging digital video, analog video, USB data, and power into a single cable. This article covers the origins and technical design of the ADC connector, explains its direct architectural relationship to Digital Visual Interface (DVI), and details why Apple eventually transitioned back to industry-standard display standards.

What Was the Apple Display Connector (ADC)?

Introduced in July 2000 alongside the Power Mac G4 and the Power Mac G4 Cube, the Apple Display Connector (ADC) was Apple’s proprietary solution to cable clutter. At the time, connecting an external monitor typically required three separate cables: a video cable to the graphics card, a power cable to an electrical outlet, and a USB cable to enable peripheral ports on the display.

Apple solved this by creating a single, heavy-duty connector that carried:

  • Digital and Analog Video: Equivalent to the DVI specification.
  • USB Data: A USB 1.1 connection to power the hub built into the monitor.
  • Power Delivery: Up to 100 watts of 25-volt DC power, eliminating the monitor's need for an independent power cord.

Monitors such as the acrylic Apple Studio Display and Apple Cinema Display relied on ADC to draw all necessary power directly from the host computer's graphics card, allowing users to turn the computer on or off using a capacitive power button on the front bezel of the display.

How ADC Related to DVI

Electrically and functionally, ADC was a repackaged, expanded version of DVI-Integrated (DVI-I).

Rather than inventing a new video transmission protocol, Apple utilized the Transition-Minimized Differential Signaling (TMDS) protocol that formed the core of standard DVI. The pinout of an ADC connector essentially placed the standard DVI-I wiring—which includes both digital TMDS channels and analog RGB lines for VGA compatibility—alongside additional pins dedicated to 25V power and USB 1.1 data.

Because the video signals were identical:

  • ADC to DVI: A computer with an ADC port could output to a standard DVI monitor using an inexpensive, passive adapter that simply routed the video pins to a standard DVI female plug, leaving the power and USB pins disconnected.
  • DVI to ADC: Connecting an ADC-equipped monitor to a standard DVI port required an active adapter. Apple released the DVI to ADC Adapter, a bulky external power brick that accepted a standard DVI signal and a USB connection, injected 25V of power, and outputted the unified signal via an ADC port.

Why Apple Retired ADC

Despite offering an elegant single-cable experience, ADC faced severe practical limitations. The requirement to deliver 100 watts of power through the graphics card placed immense thermal and electrical strain on the host Mac, requiring specialized power supplies and proprietary AGP graphics cards. It also alienated the wider PC market, as non-Apple computers could not drive ADC monitors without costly external adapters.

By June 2004, Apple began phasing out ADC with the release of the aluminum-frame Apple Cinema Displays. These newer displays returned to standard DVI connections, utilizing a captive "squid" cable breakout box that split standard DVI, USB 2.0, FireWire 400, and external power into individual connections.
