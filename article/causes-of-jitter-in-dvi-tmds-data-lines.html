Causes of Jitter in DVI TMDS Data Lines

Jitter in high-speed DVI Transition-Minimized Differential Signaling (TMDS) data lines is the temporal deviation of signal edges from their ideal arrival times, which degrades signal integrity and causes bit errors or display artifacts. In high-bandwidth video transmission, this timing variation arises from a combination of deterministic factors—such as intersymbol interference, crosstalk, and impedance mismatches—and random physical phenomena, including thermal noise and reference clock phase instability. Understanding these sources is essential for designing reliable transmitter physical layers, layout routing, and receiver equalization circuits.

Intersymbol Interference (ISI) and Cable Losses

The primary contributor to deterministic jitter in DVI TMDS lines is Intersymbol Interference (ISI) caused by frequency-dependent attenuation in the transmission medium. At high pixel clocks, skin effect resistance and dielectric losses in copper cables attenuate the high-frequency components of the digital pulses. As a result, signal edges cannot fully transition to their steady-state voltage levels within a single bit period. Previous bits influence the timing and threshold crossing of subsequent bits, causing significant data-dependent jitter.

Impedance Mismatches and Reflections

TMDS relies on a 100-ohm nominal differential impedance across the PCB traces, connectors, and cable assemblies. Any discontinuity—such as poorly designed PCB vias, solder pads, connector transitions, or low-quality cable manufacturing—creates impedance variations. These discontinuities reflect portions of the high-speed signal back and forth along the transmission line. When reflected waveforms superimpose onto incoming data transitions, they distort the signal edges and shift the differential zero-crossing points, inducing reflection-based jitter.

Crosstalk Between Differential Pairs

A standard single-link DVI interface routes four high-speed differential pairs: three color channels (Red, Green, Blue) and one clock signal. Due to tight pin spacing in DVI connectors and dense routing on printed circuit boards, electromagnetic coupling occurs between adjacent pairs. Capacitive and inductive crosstalk injects transient noise into victim lines during signal transitions on neighboring aggressor lines. This injected noise alters the instantaneous voltage of the differential pair, accelerating or delaying its threshold transitions.

Transmitter PLL Phase Noise

DVI TMDS serializes parallel pixel data using a dedicated Phase-Locked Loop (PLL) that multiplies the input pixel clock by a factor of 10. Any phase noise, thermal noise, or instability within the voltage-controlled oscillator (VCO) inside the transmitter PLL translates directly into random clock jitter. Because the serialized data transitions are clocked out using this generated signal, any timing irregularity in the internal clock is inherently transferred to the high-speed data stream.

Power Supply Induced Jitter (PSIJ)

Transmitter and receiver integrated circuits are susceptible to noise on their power distribution networks. High-speed switching within the driver circuitry or neighboring logic creates transient voltage dips and ground bounce. This power supply ripple directly affects the drive strength, switching speed, and propagation delay of the TMDS output buffers. Fluctuations in propagation delay lead to dynamic edge-timing variations known as power supply induced jitter.

Intra-Pair Skew

Intra-pair skew occurs when the two conductors comprising a single differential pair (TMDS Data+ and TMDS Data-) have unequal physical lengths or asymmetrical dielectric environments. When the inverted signals do not arrive at precisely the same time, the differential crossing point shifts. This asymmetry not only converts differential signaling into unwanted common-mode noise, but it also alters the duty cycle and broadens the transition region, increasing the overall jitter observed by the differential receiver.
