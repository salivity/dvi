DVI vs HDMI: Electrical Signaling Comparison

At their foundational physical layers, Digital Visual Interface (DVI) and High-Definition Multimedia Interface (HDMI) share virtually identical electrical signaling. Both architectures rely on Transition Minimized Differential Signaling (TMDS) to transmit uncompressed digital video data from a source to a display. Because HDMI was intentionally engineered using DVI’s core video transport mechanism as its baseline, the two standards are electrically cross-compatible for standard digital video, allowing passive physical adapters to route signals between them without active protocol conversion.

The Underlying Technology: TMDS

Both DVI and standard HDMI use TMDS on their physical layers. TMDS uses Current Mode Logic (CML) operating over balanced, DC-coupled differential twisted pairs terminated to a 3.3-volt supply.

In a standard single-link setup, both interfaces employ four differential pairs:

  • Three Data Lanes: Transmit color data (Red, Green, Blue or \(Y/Cb/Cr\)).
  • One Clock Lane: Transmits a dedicated reference clock that runs at the pixel character rate to synchronize the data transmission.

TMDS uses an 8b/10b encoding algorithm. The source converts 8-bit color bytes into 10-bit symbols designed to minimize signal transitions (reducing electromagnetic interference) and maintain DC balance across the differential lines.

Data Transmission During Blanking Intervals

The divergence between DVI and HDMI lies in how the electrical signals are utilized outside the active display area:

  • DVI Implementation: During the blanking periods (the horizontal and vertical intervals between active video frames), DVI lines transmit only 2-bit control signals, primarily representing horizontal sync (HSync) and vertical sync (VSync). The data bandwidth during blanking is essentially idle.
  • HDMI Data Islands: HDMI re-engineers the signaling during these blanking periods. Instead of idling, it switches the TMDS encoders to a specialized 4-bit to 10-bit encoding scheme (TERC4) to transmit "Data Islands." These packets carry digital audio, copy protection data, and auxiliary metadata (such as color space information and vendor-specific InfoFrames) over the exact same physical lanes.

Bandwidth, Clock Rates, and Scaling

While single-link DVI is formally standardized up to a pixel clock of 165 MHz (yielding a total TMDS throughput of 4.95 Gbps, or 3.96 Gbps usable data), HDMI expands the electrical signaling limits across its revisions:

  • HDMI 1.0–1.2: Electrically identical to single-link DVI, capped at 165 MHz.
  • HDMI 1.3–1.4: Increased the maximum TMDS clock rate to 340 MHz (10.2 Gbps total bandwidth) over the same copper topology.
  • HDMI 2.0: Pushed the TMDS character rate to 600 Mcsc (18 Gbps total bandwidth). At rates above 3.4 Gbps per channel, HDMI 2.0 introduces physical-layer data scrambling to reduce RF emissions and shifts the clock ratio from 1:10 to 1:40 to maintain stability over standard copper traces.
  • Dual-Link DVI vs. HDMI: To achieve higher resolutions, DVI implements "Dual-Link," adding three extra physical differential data lanes (a total of six data pairs and one clock pair). HDMI never adopted secondary physical lanes, instead achieving higher bandwidth exclusively by increasing the signaling frequency on the original three data lanes.

The Shift in Modern HDMI (HDMI 2.1 FRL)

The direct electrical equivalence ends with the introduction of HDMI 2.1’s Fixed Rate Link (FRL). While legacy HDMI 2.1 modes remain backwards-compatible with TMDS and DVI, native FRL replaces TMDS entirely. FRL removes the dedicated differential clock lane, repurposing it as a fourth data lane, embeds the clock directly into the data streams, and upgrades the encoding to a 16b/18b scheme. At this stage, HDMI ceases to be electrically compatible with DVI.
