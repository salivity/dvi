Can DVI Carry 3D Frame-Sequential Signals?

DVI can carry 3D stereoscopic signals using frame-sequential formatting, provided the connection has sufficient bandwidth to meet high refresh rate requirements. Because frame-sequential 3D simply delivers alternating left- and right-eye frames as a standard high-refresh-rate video stream, standard DVI interfaces—most notably Dual-Link DVI—were widely used to power early active stereoscopic PC gaming setups like NVIDIA 3D Vision.

How Frame-Sequential 3D Works Over DVI

Frame-sequential 3D, also known as active stereoscopic 3D, displays full-resolution left-eye and right-eye images consecutively. To deliver a smooth, flicker-free experience, each eye requires at least 60 frames per second, necessitating an overall display refresh rate of 120Hz or higher. Active-shutter glasses synchronize with the display to alternate between eyes in tandem with the incoming frames.

From the perspective of the video interface, frame-sequential 3D does not require specialized 3D metadata or proprietary signaling protocols. Unlike HDMI 1.4, which introduced dedicated "frame packing" 3D formats, DVI treats a frame-sequential signal as an ordinary 2D video feed running at 120Hz. The host computer's graphics card handles the rendering of alternating perspectives, while external hardware or display-integrated infrared emitters manage the synchronization of the shutter glasses.

Single-Link vs. Dual-Link DVI Requirements

Bandwidth is the primary limitation when transmitting frame-sequential 3D over DVI:

  • Single-Link DVI: Limited to a maximum pixel clock of 165 MHz, Single-Link DVI caps output at roughly 1920×1200 at 60Hz. While it can technically transmit a 120Hz frame-sequential signal at lower resolutions, such as 1280×720, it lacks the throughput for standard high-definition 3D viewing.
  • Dual-Link DVI: Employs a second data transmitter that effectively doubles the transmission bandwidth, supporting pixel clocks of 330 MHz and higher. This makes Dual-Link DVI fully capable of carrying an uncompressed 1920×1080 signal at 120Hz, fulfilling the exact requirement for full HD active stereoscopic 3D.

Practical Implementation

During the peak era of 3D PC gaming and stereoscopic CAD visualization, Dual-Link DVI-D (digital-only) was the standard interface of choice. Modern graphics cards and monitors have largely replaced DVI with DisplayPort and modern HDMI standards, both of which offer significantly higher bandwidth and built-in audio support. However, on supported legacy hardware, a Dual-Link DVI connection remains a functional and native method for transmitting frame-sequential 3D video.
