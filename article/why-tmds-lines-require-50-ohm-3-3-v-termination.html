Why TMDS Lines Require 50-Ohm 3.3V Termination

Transition Minimized Differential Signaling (TMDS) is the physical-layer protocol used in high-speed digital video standards such as HDMI and DVI. Reliable data transmission across TMDS lines depends on a specific termination scheme at the sink (receiver): each single-ended line must be pulled up to a +3.3V DC rail through a 50-ohm resistor. This configuration is necessary to supply operating current for the transmitter's Current Mode Logic (CML) architecture, establish precise differential logic levels, and eliminate signal reflections across the transmission line.

Current Mode Logic (CML) Driver Architecture

TMDS transmitters do not utilize push-pull output stages that alternate between driving power and ground. Instead, they employ an open-collector (or open-drain) Current Mode Logic (CML) differential driver consisting of switched current sinks.

Because the transmitter can only pull current down to ground and cannot source current, the receiver must provide the electrical path to pull the lines up. Terminating the lines to +3.3V (often designated as \(AV_{CC}\)) at the receiver supplies the necessary operating voltage and current loop required for the circuit to function.

Generating Digital Voltage Swings

The combination of the +3.3V supply and the 50-ohm load resistor sets the exact signal swing required by TMDS specifications:

  • Logic High State: When the transmitter switch for a specific line is turned OFF, no current flows through the corresponding 50-ohm resistor. With zero current drop across the resistor (\(V = I \times R\)), the line voltage rests at the supply level of +3.3V.
  • Logic Low State: When the transmitter switch turns ON, it sinks a nominal current of approximately 10 mA through the line. Across the 50-ohm resistor, this generates a voltage drop of: \[V_{drop} = 10\text{ mA} \times 50\ \Omega = 500\text{ mV}\] The line voltage drops from +3.3V down to +2.8V.

This creates a single-ended peak-to-peak swing of roughly 500 mV (or a 1.0V peak-to-peak differential swing between the paired lines) centered around a common-mode voltage of approximately 3.05V, which the receiver's differential comparators are designed to decode.

Transmission Line Impedance Matching

TMDS carries high-frequency signals exceeding multiple gigabits per second per lane. At these frequencies, copper traces on printed circuit boards (PCBs) and the twisted-pair cables act as transmission lines rather than simple conductors.

HDMI and DVI specifications define the characteristic impedance (\(Z_0\)) of TMDS lines as:

  • 50 ohms single-ended (measured to ground/reference).
  • 100 ohms differential (measured across the pair).

If an incoming electrical wave encounters an impedance mismatch when it arrives at the receiver, a portion of the wave energy reflects back toward the transmitter. These reflections bounce between the transmitter and receiver, corrupting signal edges, introducing phase jitter, and closing the data "eye" diagram.

Placing a 50-ohm resistor between each line and the AC ground (+3.3V rail acting as an AC ground via decoupling capacitors) creates a matched termination. The incoming energy is fully absorbed by the termination network, preventing line reflections and preserving waveform integrity over cable runs.

Receiver Detection and Sink Presence

Beyond signal transmission, the +3.3V pull-up network acts as a passive sink-detection mechanism. When an HDMI source is plugged into an active display, the source's TMDS output pins detect the DC bias created by the receiver's pull-up resistors. This confirms that the connected sink device is powered on, properly terminated, and physically ready to receive high-speed video data.
