How Crimped DVI Cables Cause Signal Reflections

A crimped DVI cable introduces physical deformation that alters the electrical characteristics of its internal conductors, leading to an impedance discontinuity along the transmission line. In high-speed digital connections like DVI, signals behave as high-frequency electromagnetic waves that require a uniform impedance—typically 100 ohms across differential pairs—to propagate smoothly. When the cable is bent or crushed, this uniform pathway is broken, causing a portion of the traveling electrical wave to bounce back toward the source as a signal reflection. This reflected energy interferes with the incoming data, resulting in visual artifacts, pixel jitter, or complete loss of the video display.

High-Speed Signals and Characteristic Impedance

DVI (Digital Visual Interface) relies on Transition Minimized Differential Signaling (TMDS) to transmit high-definition video data at frequencies often exceeding 1 GHz. At these high frequencies, the wires inside the cable act as transmission lines rather than simple low-resistance conductors.

A transmission line is defined by its characteristic impedance (\(Z_0\)), which depends directly on the physical geometry of the cable:

  • The diameter of the copper conductors
  • The spacing between the two wires in a differential pair
  • The dielectric constant of the insulating material separating them

As long as the geometry remains strictly uniform from end to end, the impedance remains constant, and the digital signals travel smoothly from the graphics card to the display without energy loss.

Physical Deformation from Crimping

Crimping or sharply bending a DVI cable physically alters the internal geometry of the shielded twisted pairs. When the cable is crushed:

  1. Conductor Spacing Changes: The distance between the paired wires decreases at the pinch point.
  2. Dielectric Compression: The insulating material is compressed, altering its local dielectric properties.
  3. Shielding Distortion: The outer foil or braided shielding can be warped, altering the parasitic capacitance between the conductors and ground.

Because characteristic impedance is fundamentally governed by the ratio of inductance (\(L\)) to capacitance (\(C\)) per unit length (\(Z_0 = \sqrt{L/C}\)), decreasing the spacing between conductors increases the capacitance while lowering the inductance. Consequently, the crimped section experiences a sharp drop in local impedance.

The Physics of Signal Reflection

When an electromagnetic signal encounters a sudden shift in impedance, it reaches an electrical boundary condition. High-speed digital signals cannot instantly adjust to this discontinuity. To satisfy Maxwell's equations and the conservation of energy at the boundary, the signal splits into two components:

  • Transmitted wave: The portion of the signal that continues past the crimp, though with distorted amplitude and phase.
  • Reflected wave: The portion of the signal that reflects off the discontinuity and travels backward toward the transmitter.

The magnitude of this reflection is governed by the reflection coefficient (\(\Gamma\)):

\[\Gamma = \frac{Z_L - Z_0}{Z_L + Z_0}\]

Where \(Z_0\) is the nominal impedance of the undamaged cable (100 ohms) and \(Z_L\) is the altered impedance at the crimp. If the crimp drops the impedance to 75 ohms, \(\Gamma\) becomes negative, creating an inverted reflected wave that bounces back toward the transmitter.

Consequences for Digital Video Signals

As the reflected wave bounces back along the cable, it collides with subsequent forward-traveling digital pulses. This interaction causes several distinct signal integrity problems:

  • Destructive Interference and Attenuation: The reflected wave overlaps with incoming pulses, altering their voltage levels. This closes the "eye diagram" (the open window where a receiver can reliably read high and low digital states).
  • Signal Jitter: Distorted edge transitions make it difficult for the receiver's clock recovery circuit to determine precisely when a bit changes from 0 to 1, causing timing synchronization errors.
  • Secondary Reflections: If the transmitter end is not perfectly matched to absorb the reflected wave, the signal can reflect a second time, bouncing forward again toward the monitor as a delayed "ghost" pulse.

At the user level, these high-frequency reflections corrupt the raw pixel data. Because DVI does not feature packet-level error correction like newer standards, corrupted bits manifest immediately on the monitor as flickering "sparkles" (stray colored pixels), periodic flashing, reduced resolution limits, or a total failure of the monitor to lock onto the video signal.
