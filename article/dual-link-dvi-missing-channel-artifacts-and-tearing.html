Dual-Link DVI Missing Channel Artifacts and Tearing

When a dual-link DVI connection loses one or more of its secondary data channels, the resulting visual output exhibits severe image corruption, most notably vertical column interleaving, horizontal tearing, and color-specific artifacting. This article explains how the dual-link DVI architecture distributes pixel data, why missing or damaged physical channels manifest as distinct "interlaced" or torn visual patterns, and how to identify whether a display anomaly stems from a hardware channel failure.

The Architecture of Dual-Link DVI

To understand the visual defects, it is necessary to examine how dual-link DVI handles bandwidth. A single DVI link uses three Transition-Minimized Differential Signaling (TMDS) data channels (carrying Red, Green, and Blue) and one clock channel, supporting up to 1920×1200 at 60 Hz.

To support higher resolutions (such as 2560×1600) or higher refresh rates, dual-link DVI introduces a second set of three TMDS data channels (pins 4, 5, 12, 13, 20, and 21) while sharing the same continuous clock line.

Instead of splitting the image into top and bottom halves, the DVI specification uses an alternating pixel architecture:

  • Link 1 transmits odd-numbered horizontal pixels (1, 3, 5, etc.).
  • Link 2 transmits even-numbered horizontal pixels (2, 4, 6, etc.).

Vertical "Interlacing" Artifacts (The Comb Effect)

The most prominent visual defect caused by missing dual-link channels is vertical striping, often mistakenly referred to as interlacing.

Traditional interlacing alternates horizontal scanlines over time. However, when the secondary link fails completely due to a bent pin, broken wire, or faulty multiplexer:

  • The display panel attempts to assemble the full resolution image using data from both links.
  • Link 1 correctly renders every odd pixel column.
  • Link 2 provides no data, zeroed values, or random noise for every even pixel column.

This results in a distinct "jailbar," "comb," or "pinstripe" effect across the entire panel. Every other vertical line of pixels will appear pitch black, solid bright, or stuck at a static color. While this visually mimics an interlaced video stream that has failed to de-interlace, the pattern is oriented vertically rather than horizontally.

Micro-Tearing and Phase Desynchronization

When dual-link channels are partially damaged or suffer from severe signal degradation (attenuation or skew), true tearing occurs across the screen.

Because both links rely on a single shared clock, any propagation delay or phase shift between Link 1 and Link 2 creates timing mismatches inside the display’s receiver controller. If the receiver cannot synchronize the pixel pairs within the allowed horizontal blanking interval:

  • Horizontal Pixel Shearing: Individual pixel pairs fall out of alignment, causing fine, microscopic horizontal tears along high-contrast edges and text.
  • Buffer Swapping Tears: Displays buffering frames internally may update odd pixel columns on a different frame cycle than even pixel columns. During rapid on-screen movement, this causes temporal shearing where moving objects appear simultaneously in two different spatial locations across alternating vertical columns.

Color-Specific Banding and "Sparkling"

A failure does not always take down the entire secondary link; it can isolate a single sub-channel:

  • Missing Color Data: If only the second Red channel (Channel 3) is severed, the display still receives Green and Blue data for even pixels. Consequently, every second column will lack red intensity, causing the entire display to show alternating stripes of normal color and heavy cyan or green tints.
  • Pixel Sparkle/Snow: A marginal connection that intermittently drops differential voltage levels causes high-frequency noise. Because the secondary link handles alternating pixels, this noise manifests as localized "sparkling" or shimmering confined strictly to even-numbered pixel columns.

Resolution Fallback vs. Raw Artifacting

Modern monitors and graphics cards negotiate link configurations via Display Data Channel (DDC) and Extended Display Identification Data (EDID).

If the GPU detects during the handshake that the secondary link is completely non-functional, it may automatically prevent these visual tearing and interleaving effects by downscaling the output to a standard single-link resolution (such as 1920×1080 at 60 Hz). The severe vertical interleaving and tearing artifacts primarily appear when the GPU forcefully transmits a dual-link raster across a physically degraded cable or when pins detach while a high-resolution session is actively running.
