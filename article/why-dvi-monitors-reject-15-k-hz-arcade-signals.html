Why DVI Monitors Reject 15 kHz Arcade Signals

Standard digital DVI monitors reject 15 kHz arcade video signals primarily due to incompatible horizontal scan rates, pixel clock minimum thresholds mandated by the DVI specification, and differing synchronization standards. Classic arcade hardware outputs low-resolution analog video designed for cathode-ray tubes (CRTs), while modern digital computer displays adhere strictly to VESA digital timings. Without an active intermediate upscaler or line doubler to bridge these protocol and timing gaps, a modern DVI monitor cannot sync, process, or display the incoming arcade signal, resulting in an "Out of Range" or "No Signal" error.

Horizontal Scan Rate Incompatibility

The primary obstacle between arcade hardware and DVI displays is the horizontal scan frequency (\(f_H\)). Classic arcade systems (such as JAMMA-era boards, Neo Geo, and Capcom CPS systems) output video designed for standard-resolution CRT monitors. These systems operate at a nominal horizontal scan rate of approximately 15.7 kHz to generate 240p or 480i video at 60 Hz.

Conversely, PC monitors—and the DVI standard derived from PC display norms—trace their heritage to the VESA VGA standard. VGA established a minimum baseline horizontal scan frequency of 31.5 kHz (640x480 at 60 Hz). Digital monitor scalers and firmware are explicitly designed to monitor incoming scan rates, automatically rejecting anything that falls below this roughly 30–31 kHz threshold to prevent timing errors.

The TMDS Minimum Pixel Clock Threshold

Digital Visual Interface (DVI) relies on Transition-Minimized Differential Signaling (TMDS) to transmit high-speed digital data. According to the official DVI 1.0 specification, the TMDS link features a hard minimum pixel clock frequency:

  • Standard DVI Minimum Clock: 25.175 MHz
  • Standard 15 kHz Arcade Pixel Clock: Typically between 5 MHz and 8 MHz

A 15 kHz arcade signal outputs far too few pixels per second to meet the lower operational limit of the monitor's TMDS receiver hardware. When a digital receiver chip receives a clock frequency below ~25 MHz, its Phase-Locked Loop (PLL) circuits cannot lock onto the signal, rendering the monitor incapable of reconstructing the image data.

Analog-to-Digital and Pinout Mismatches

Arcade boards output analog RGBS (Red, Green, Blue, and a shared Sync line). DVI connectors come in several varieties: DVI-D (digital-only), DVI-A (analog-only), and DVI-I (integrated digital and analog).

Most modern DVI monitors are strictly DVI-D. Passing an arcade signal via a passive physical adapter directly to a DVI-D port fails immediately because the monitor lacks an internal analog-to-digital converter (ADC) on that port. Even on monitors equipped with DVI-I inputs that do accept analog signals, the internal scaler still applies the strict 31.5 kHz minimum scan rate rule to the analog pins, dropping the 15 kHz feed.

Sync Signal Incompatibilities

The synchronization formats also conflict:

  1. Format: Arcade platforms typically combine horizontal and vertical sync into a single Composite Sync (C-Sync) line. Standard PC displays expect separate Horizontal Sync (H-Sync) and Vertical Sync (V-Sync) lines.
  2. Voltage Levels: Arcade video hardware often outputs "TTL-level" sync signals, which can peak at around 5V. VESA standards for analog RGB lines generally expect standard video signals to remain around 0.7V peak-to-peak for color channels and lower TTL levels for sync, which can overload or confuse consumer monitor circuits.

How to Resolve the Incompatibility

Because standard DVI monitors cannot process native 15 kHz signals, bridging the gap requires an active scan converter or line doubler (such as an OSSC, RetroTINK, or GBS-Control). These devices sample the 15 kHz analog lines, double each scanline in memory to boost the horizontal frequency to at least 31.5 kHz (480p), serialize the data to meet the 25 MHz+ TMDS clock requirement, and output a compatible digital format that standard DVI displays accept.
