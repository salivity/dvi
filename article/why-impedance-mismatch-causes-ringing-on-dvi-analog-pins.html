Why Impedance Mismatch Causes Ringing on DVI Analog Pins

This article explains how impedance discontinuities at the physical connector interface cause signal reflections that manifest as high-frequency ringing on DVI analog pins. When the characteristic impedance of a transmission path shifts abruptly at the connector, the high-speed analog video signals driving Red, Green, and Blue channels reflect back and forth along the cable. This interaction results in overshoot, undershoot, and damped oscillations—commonly known as ringing—which ultimately degrade display quality by causing visual artifacts like ghosting and edge fringing.

The 75-Ohm Transmission Standard

Analog DVI implementations—specifically DVI-A and the analog pins of DVI-I (pins C1 through C4 carrying Red, Green, Blue, and Horizontal Sync)—are designed around a strict 75-ohm characteristic impedance (\(Z_0\)). Video Digital-to-Analog Converters (DACs) at the graphics card output a controlled signal, typically terminated with 75-ohm source and load resistances to allow maximum power transfer without signal distortion.

As pixel resolutions and refresh rates increase, the pixel clock frequencies reach well beyond 100 MHz, resulting in signal rise times measured in nanoseconds or picoseconds. At these speeds, PCB traces, connector pins, and cables behave strictly as high-frequency transmission lines rather than simple conductors.

How Connector Discontinuities Cause Reflections

A connector introduces a physical change in geometry, conductor spacing, and dielectric materials compared to the printed circuit board (PCB) traces and the shielded coaxial lines within the DVI cable. If the connector is poorly designed, poorly shielded, or improperly soldered, its local characteristic impedance deviates from the target 75 ohms.

When a high-frequency voltage step encounters this impedance boundary, it cannot pass through seamlessly. According to transmission line theory, the amplitude of the reflected wave relative to the incident wave is determined by the reflection coefficient (\(\Gamma\)):

\[\Gamma = \frac{Z_L - Z_0}{Z_L + Z_0}\]

Where:

  • \(Z_0\) is the characteristic impedance of the transmission line.
  • \(Z_L\) is the localized impedance of the connector.

If \(Z_L\) does not equal \(Z_0\), \(\Gamma\) is non-zero. A fraction of the signal's energy reflects back toward the source, while the remainder continues toward the display load.

The Transition from Reflection to Ringing

Ringing is not a single reflection, but the result of continuous, multiple reflections bouncing between impedance discontinuities:

  1. Edge Transition: The video DAC generates a sharp edge transition (representing an abrupt change in pixel brightness or color).
  2. First Reflection: As the wavefront reaches the connector mismatch, part of the energy reflects back toward the source.
  3. Secondary Reflections: If the source termination is not perfectly matched across all frequencies, the reflected wave reflects again off the source and travels forward a second time.
  4. Resonance and Damped Oscillation: Because parasitic capacitance and lead inductance are naturally present in the pins and mating contacts of the DVI connector, the connector forms a localized LC resonant circuit. The energy trapped between the boundaries oscillates at the resonant frequency of this LC network.

These rapid, decaying oscillations superimpose onto the flat top and bottom of the original square or trapezoidal video pulses. This damped sinusoidal disturbance is what is measured on an oscilloscope as ringing, characterized by overshoot (spiking above the intended voltage) and undershoot (dropping below it).

Visual Impact on the Display

Because the analog DVI pins directly determine pixel luminance via voltage levels (typically 0V to 0.7V), ringing directly translates into visible display defects:

  • Ghosting and Halos: Sharp transitions between light and dark areas exhibit faint repeating lines or glowing fringes immediately to the right of vertical edges.
  • Loss of Text Crispness: Ringing destabilizes the settle time of individual pixels, causing fonts and fine details to appear blurred or smeared.
  • Color Bleed: Because each color channel has its own analog pin, impedance variations among individual pins cause phase-shifted ringing, creating false color fringes around high-contrast borders.
