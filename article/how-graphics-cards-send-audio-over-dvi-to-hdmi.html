How Graphics Cards Send Audio Over DVI to HDMI

While the official Digital Visual Interface (DVI) specification only defines video transmission, modern graphics card manufacturers successfully bypassed this limitation to deliver digital sound through standard DVI ports. By leveraging the shared electrical architecture between DVI and HDMI, graphics processing units (GPUs) dynamically switch the transmission protocol on the DVI port to output an HDMI-compliant signal. This approach embeds packetized audio within standard video blanking intervals, allowing a completely passive DVI-to-HDMI adapter to route both video and multichannel audio to an HDMI display.

The foundation of this capability lies in Transition-Minimized Differential Signaling (TMDS). Both single-link DVI and HDMI utilize identical physical signaling layers, consisting of three data channels and one clock channel. Because HDMI was designed to be backward-compatible with DVI at the electrical level, the physical wiring of single-link DVI and standard HDMI is virtually identical.

The original DVI standard only accounts for active pixel transmission during the display refresh cycle. HDMI, however, introduces "Data Island" periods. During the horizontal and vertical blanking intervals—the brief pauses when an electronic display moves from the end of one scan line or frame to the beginning of the next—HDMI pauses pixel transmission and sends packetized data instead. These data packets carry ancillary information, including multi-channel digital audio formats such as LPCM, Dolby Digital, and DTS.

Graphics card manufacturers realized that the physical DVI port was entirely capable of carrying these data islands if the GPU ignored strict DVI transmission rules. When a display is connected, the graphics card queries the monitor’s Extended Display Identification Data (EDID). If the EDID reports that the connected screen is an HDMI-compliant sink capable of receiving audio, the GPU switches its output protocol. Rather than driving standard DVI signals, the GPU outputs a full HDMI stream directly through the DVI connector pins. Because the signal is already natively formatted as HDMI, the physical adapter requires no active conversion hardware; it merely reroutes the physical pins from a DVI layout to an HDMI plug.

Hardware implementations evolved over several GPU generations to make this seamless:

  • AMD (ATI): Beginning with the Radeon HD 2000 series, AMD integrated a dedicated High Definition Audio controller directly onto the graphics silicon. Early implementations required a proprietary DVI-to-HDMI dongle equipped with an internal EEPROM chip. The GPU driver read this chip to verify that an approved adapter was attached before enabling HDMI signaling and audio output over the DVI pins. Later generations dropped the proprietary dongle requirement in favor of standard passive adapters.
  • NVIDIA: NVIDIA initially tackled audio over DVI using an internal two-pin S/PDIF pass-through cable connected from the motherboard or sound card directly to headers on cards like the GeForce 9000 and GTX 200 series. The GPU multiplexed this external digital audio stream into the TMDS data blanking intervals on the DVI port. Starting with the GeForce 400 (Fermi) series, NVIDIA followed AMD’s lead by integrating on-die HD audio processors, eliminating internal cables altogether.

Through this combination of shared TMDS physical wiring, protocol-switching display drivers, and integrated audio processing, modern GPUs treat the DVI port as an HDMI port in a different physical form factor, making audio transmission fully functional without altering the legacy connector.
