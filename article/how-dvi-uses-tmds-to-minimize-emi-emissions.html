How DVI Uses TMDS to Minimize EMI Emissions

Digital Visual Interface (DVI) relies on Transition-Minimized Differential Signaling (TMDS) to transmit high-speed digital video while significantly reducing electromagnetic interference (EMI). High-frequency digital signals naturally emit electromagnetic radiation that can disrupt nearby electronics and fail regulatory standards. By combining an intelligent 8-to-10-bit encoding algorithm with differential physical signaling, TMDS suppresses the electromagnetic radiation typically generated by high-bandwidth data transfers over copper cables.

The Core Technology: TMDS

The signaling architecture used by DVI is Transition-Minimized Differential Signaling, developed by Silicon Image. TMDS operates through a two-stage process: an advanced encoding algorithm implemented at the transmitter, followed by low-voltage differential transmission across twisted-pair wiring.

Minimizing Signal Transitions

Standard binary data often contains frequent transitions between logic low (0) and logic high (1). Rapid voltage swings generate sharp edge rates (high \(dv/dt\)), which produce significant high-frequency harmonics responsible for EMI emissions.

TMDS tackles this at the logical level using an 8b/10b encoding algorithm:

  1. Transition Reduction: The encoder takes 8-bit pixel data and determines whether an XOR or XNOR operation will result in fewer transitions between adjacent bits. It converts the byte into a 9-bit code where the 9th bit indicates which operation was performed. By minimizing transitions, the signal reduces high-frequency spectral content.
  2. DC Balancing: A 10th bit is added to maintain direct current (DC) balance. The encoder tracks the cumulative difference between logic 1s and logic 0s transmitted over the line. If an imbalance develops, the 10th bit selectively inverts the data word to keep the long-term average voltage centered. This prevents baseline wander and eliminates low-frequency harmonic spikes.

Differential Signaling and Noise Cancellation

Once encoded, the 10-bit data is transmitted serially over a shielded twisted pair using differential signaling:

  • Opposing Polarities: The transmitter sends the true signal along one conductor and an exact inverted (complementary) signal along the adjacent conductor.
  • Magnetic Field Cancellation: Because equal currents flow in opposite directions through closely coupled conductors, the magnetic fields generated by the two wires cancel each other out. This drastically reduces the electromagnetic radiation escaping the cable.
  • Common-Mode Rejection: At the receiving end, a differential receiver measures the voltage difference between the two conductors rather than referencing a shared ground. Any external noise that couples into the cable affects both conductors equally and is rejected by the receiver, preserving signal integrity without requiring excessive shielding.

Through this combination of logic-level transition suppression, DC balancing, and complementary physical signaling, TMDS allows DVI connections to sustain multi-gigabit data rates with minimal EMI emissions.
