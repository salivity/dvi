How Current-Mode Logic Minimizes DVI Switching Noise

Digital Visual Interface (DVI) hardware relies on Current-Mode Logic (CML) within its Transition-Minimized Differential Signaling (TMDS) architecture to maintain signal integrity at high data rates. By steering a constant current across differential lines rather than rapidly charging and discharging capacitive loads, CML fundamentally alters how signals propagate. This architectural choice suppresses power-supply ripple, minimizes ground bounce, lowers electromagnetic emissions, and eliminates high-frequency ringing across high-speed video channels.

Elimination of Power Supply Current Spikes (\(dI/dt\))

Standard voltage-mode logic (such as CMOS) draws sharp, instantaneous bursts of current from the power supply whenever a state transition occurs, as parasitic capacitances are rapidly charged and discharged. In high-speed interfaces, these rapid rate-of-change transitions (\(dI/dt\)) interact with parasitic bond-wire and trace inductances, generating severe power rail fluctuations and ground bounce.

CML resolves this by using an active constant-current sink connected to a differential pair of transistors. Instead of switching power on and off, the circuit continuously draws a steady current and merely steers it between the true and inverted signal paths. Because the total current drawn from the supply rail remains virtually static regardless of the data being transmitted, \(dI/dt\) is virtually zero, eliminating the primary source of supply-induced switching noise.

Reduced Voltage Swing

Conventional logic families swing between ground and the supply voltage (e.g., 0V to 3.3V), generating high voltage slew rates (\(dV/dt\)) that induce capacitive crosstalk into adjacent circuit traces. In contrast, CML operates with a substantially reduced differential voltage swing—typically around 400 mV to 600 mV per line, referenced to a 3.3V termination rail in DVI implementations. Lowering the total voltage transition dramatically dampens the electric field fluctuations, reducing both near-end and far-end capacitive crosstalk between neighboring TMDS clock and data channels.

Common-Mode Cancellation and EMI Suppression

CML drivers transmit data across tightly coupled differential trace pairs. When current switches from one line to the other, the resulting electromagnetic fields are equal in magnitude but opposite in phase. These opposing fields effectively cancel each other out in the far field, minimizing radiated electromagnetic interference (EMI). Furthermore, any external noise injected into the transmission medium couples into both lines equally as common-mode noise. Because the receiver evaluates only the differential voltage across the pair, this common-mode noise is inherently rejected.

Controlled Transmission Line Termination

DVI receivers terminate each TMDS differential line with a 50-ohm resistor pulled up to the supply voltage. CML integrates naturally with this termination scheme by using the load resistors directly to establish the signal's logic levels (\(V_{swing} = I_{tail} \times R_{load}\)). Matching the source and receiver impedance directly to the characteristic impedance of the transmission line prevents signal reflections. Without reflections, the circuit avoids high-frequency voltage overshoot and ringing, which would otherwise introduce jitter and high-frequency switching noise into the DVI transceiver.
