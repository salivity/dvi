Matrox MMS Series Multi-DVI Cards Explained

The Matrox MMS (Multi-Monitor Series) was a line of specialized graphics cards engineered in the late 1990s and early 2000s to drive multi-screen workstations from a single expansion slot. Primarily deployed in mission-critical environments such as financial trading floors, medical monitoring, and dispatch centers, these cards bypassed the physical and technological limits of their era to output up to four digital or analog displays simultaneously. By combining multiple onboard graphics processors with high-density breakout connections, the MMS family established an early standard for multi-display computing long before modern multi-port GPUs existed.

What Was the Matrox MMS Series?

The Matrox MMS series was designed specifically for 2D multi-screen reliability rather than 3D gaming performance. Built around existing Matrox architectures—notably the G200 and G450 graphics chips—these boards were released primarily in PCI and PCI-X form factors.

During this era, operating systems and standard consumer motherboards struggled to handle multiple discrete graphics cards simultaneously without driver conflicts or resource allocation issues. Matrox resolved this by creating single-slot boards—such as the Matrox G200 MMS and G450 MMS—that could run two to four independent displays. Multiple MMS cards could also be combined within a single system to drive up to sixteen synchronized monitors without third-party display management hardware.

How the MMS Series Provided Multiple DVI Ports

Providing up to four DVI ports on a single-slot expansion card presented two major challenges: silicon throughput and physical bracket space. Matrox solved both through a combination of multi-chip board design and proprietary cabling interfaces.

1. Multi-Controller Architecture

Standard graphics processing units of the time were limited to driving one or, at most, two displays. To achieve quad-output capability, Matrox placed multiple discrete graphics chips on a single printed circuit board (PCB):

  • PCI-to-PCI Bridging: The card utilized an onboard PCI-to-PCI bridge chip. This allowed the system's motherboard to see just one device slot, while the card internally distributed bandwidth across two or four independent graphics processors.
  • Dedicated Framebuffers: Each onboard controller had its own dedicated memory (SDRAM or DDR), preventing bandwidth contention when rendering multiple high-resolution desktop environments simultaneously.

2. High-Density Connectors (LFH-60)

A standard full-height PCI bracket is only tall enough to fit at most two standard DVI connectors, and single-slot space constraints made four native DVI ports physically impossible.

To overcome this mechanical limitation, Matrox utilized high-density connectors directly on the card bracket, most commonly the LFH-60 (Low Force Helix 60-pin) interface:

  • Dual-LFH Layout: A quad-port card featured two LFH-60 female ports on its rear bracket. Each LFH-60 connector carried the video signals for two fully independent display channels.
  • Breakout Y-Cables: Users connected specialized breakout cables (often called "octopus" or "Y-cables") to each LFH port. Each cable split the 60-pin connection into two standard, full-sized DVI-I female connectors.
  • Digital and Analog Flexibility: Because the pinouts carried integrated DVI-I signals, the breakout cables could supply either native digital DVI signals to early flat panels or convert them via passive adapters to standard analog VGA (D-Sub) monitors.

Through this combination of an onboard bridge routing multiple GPUs and high-density pinout breakout cables, the Matrox MMS series successfully delivered up to four fully independent DVI outputs through a single PCI slot.
