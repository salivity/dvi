Why DVI Uses Current-Mode Logic Instead of CMOS

Digital Visual Interface (DVI) relies on Transition-Minimized Differential Signaling (TMDS), a standard implemented using Current-Mode Logic (CML) rather than conventional Complementary Metal-Oxide-Semiconductor (CMOS) voltage-level switching. This architectural choice is essential for transmitting uncompressed, high-definition digital video across physical cables at gigabit speeds. Standard CMOS logic suffers from severe high-frequency limitations, whereas CML provides superior signal integrity, low electromagnetic interference, clean impedance matching, and reliable transmission over extended distances.

Slew Rates and Switching Speeds

Standard CMOS circuits represent binary states by driving output lines rail-to-rail, typically swinging between 0 volts and 3.3 or 5 volts. Charging and discharging the parasitic capacitance of long traces and cables over such a broad voltage range requires significant time, severely limiting the maximum slew rate. At the multi-gigabit data rates demanded by DVI (up to 1.65 Gbps per channel for single-link), CMOS signals distort into unintelligible waveforms. CML solves this problem by using a low-voltage differential swing—typically only 400 to 500 millivolts. This small swing allows for extremely sharp rise and fall times, enabling the high switching frequencies required for digital video.

Reduced Electromagnetic Interference and Ground Bounce

Rail-to-rail CMOS switching creates sharp current spikes whenever output transistors toggle between power and ground rails. Over a copper cable, these sudden current demands create substantial electromagnetic radiation (EMI) and power supply noise, commonly called "ground bounce." CML, in contrast, steers a constant bias current between two differential conductors. Because the total current drawn by the transmitter remains steady regardless of whether the bit is a 0 or a 1, power rail fluctuations are virtually eliminated, drastically reducing radiated emissions and preventing cross-talk between adjacent color channels.

Transmission Line Termination and Impedance Matching

High-frequency signals behave as wave transmissions rather than simple DC connections. To prevent signal reflections that cause ghosting and bit errors, the transmission line must be terminated with a resistance that exactly matches its characteristic impedance. CML integrates naturally into this environment. A CML transmitter is open-drain (or open-collector), relying on precision 50-ohm pull-up resistors located at the receiver end. This architecture terminates the differential pair into a clean 100-ohm differential impedance directly at the destination, absorbing the traveling energy completely and maintaining an open, readable data "eye" diagram.

Common-Mode Noise Rejection

Standard CMOS connections are usually single-ended, making them vulnerable to external electromagnetic interference from nearby electronics and power lines. CML inherently operates differentially: the data is carried as the voltage difference between two complementary lines. Any external noise that couples into the twisted pair affects both conductors equally. The differential receiver subtracts one signal from the other, canceling out the common-mode noise while doubling the effective amplitude of the intended data signal.

Dynamic Power Efficiency at High Frequencies

Although CMOS is famous for near-zero static power consumption, its dynamic power dissipation scales quadratically with voltage and linearly with frequency (\(P = C \cdot V^2 \cdot f\)). At gigahertz frequencies, driving a capacitive cable with full CMOS voltage swings consumes massive amounts of power and generates unmanageable heat. CML trades away CMOS's low static power for a fixed, predictable power profile. Because its voltage swing is tiny, CML uses far less dynamic power at high transmission speeds, making it the superior technical choice for the DVI standard.
