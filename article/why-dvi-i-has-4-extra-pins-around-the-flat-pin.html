Why DVI-I Has 4 Extra Pins Around the Flat Pin

The primary reason a DVI-I connector features four extra pins surrounding its flat blade while a DVI-D connector does not comes down to signal compatibility. While both standards carry pure digital video signals, DVI-I is designed to transmit legacy analog signals as well. This article explains the specific function of those four extra pins, why DVI-D omits them, and how this physical difference impacts hardware compatibility.

Understanding DVI-I vs. DVI-D

The Digital Visual Interface (DVI) standard was created during the transition period from cathode-ray tube (CRT) monitors to flat-panel LCD screens. To accommodate both older analog displays and newer digital monitors, manufacturers developed two distinct connection types:

  • DVI-D (Digital): Supports strictly digital video signals.
  • DVI-I (Integrated): Supports both digital and analog video signals within the same connector.

The Purpose of the Four Extra Pins

The wide, flat blade on the side of a DVI connector is known as the ground bar. In a DVI-I connector, the four small pins placed directly above and below this flat blade (labeled C1 through C4) are dedicated exclusively to transmitting analog video:

  • Pin C1: Analog Red signal
  • Pin C2: Analog Green signal
  • Pin C3: Analog Blue signal
  • Pin C4: Analog Horizontal Sync
  • Flat Blade (C5): Analog Ground / Return

These five elements replicate the exact signals found inside a standard 15-pin VGA cable. Because DVI-D is an exclusively digital connection, it has no use for analog RGB signals. Consequently, DVI-D cables and ports completely omit these four pins, featuring only the flat ground blade alongside the standard digital pin grid.

Why the Physical Difference Matters

The physical presence or absence of these four pins serves as a mechanical key to prevent incompatible connections:

  1. VGA Compatibility: Passive DVI-to-VGA adapters rely entirely on the four analog pins of a DVI-I port. Because a DVI-D port lacks the internal wiring and physical pinholes for these pins, a passive VGA adapter cannot be plugged into a DVI-D port.
  2. Cable Fitment: A DVI-D cable will easily plug into a DVI-I port because the DVI-I female socket has holes to accept both pin configurations. However, a DVI-I cable cannot physically plug into a DVI-D port because the solid face of the DVI-D female port blocks the four analog pins.

In summary, the four pins around the flat blade exist entirely to carry analog VGA-compatible signals. DVI-I includes them to offer backwards compatibility with analog monitors, while DVI-D leaves them out to save on manufacturing costs and simplify purely digital connections.
