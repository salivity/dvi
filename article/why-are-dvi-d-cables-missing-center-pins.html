Why Are DVI-D Cables Missing Center Pins?

Many users notice that their DVI-D cables have a distinct gap in the middle of the pin grid, missing six specific pins found on fully populated connectors. This article explains the technical differences between single-link and dual-link DVI standards, the function of those omitted pins, and why manufacturers deliberately leave them out to reduce costs while maintaining seamless backward compatibility.

Single-Link vs. Dual-Link DVI

The presence or absence of the center pins designates whether a cable is DVI-D Single-Link or DVI-D Dual-Link. Digital Visual Interface (DVI) transmits data using Transition-Minimized Differential Signaling (TMDS).

  • Single-Link DVI utilizes three TMDS data channels (red, green, and blue) alongside one clock channel. This configuration requires only 18 digital pins and provides enough bandwidth to drive displays up to 1920×1200 resolution at 60 Hz.
  • Dual-Link DVI adds a second set of three data channels, doubling the maximum bandwidth to support resolutions up to 2560×1600 at 60 Hz or 1080p at 144 Hz. This setup requires all 24 digital pins arranged in a solid 3×8 grid.

Why the Center Pins Are Omitted

The six center pins (specifically pins 4, 5, 12, 13, 19, and 20) house the second TMDS data link. Cables that lack these pins are strictly single-link cables. Manufacturers omit them for practical reasons:

  1. Manufacturing Cost and Material Efficiency: Producing an internal cable assembly with six fewer shielded copper conductors lowers production costs significantly. Omitting the physical metal pins on the plug avoids wasting raw materials on pins that have no corresponding wires inside the jacket.
  2. Preventing Misrepresentation: Including the center pins without soldering them to internal wires would create a "dummy" dual-link connector. A user plugging this cable into a 1440p or 144 Hz monitor would experience blank screens or severe artifacts because the display would detect dual-link hardware where only single-link bandwidth exists. Leaving the pin block visibly empty provides immediate clarity about the cable's capabilities.
  3. Cable Flexibility and Weight: Dual-link cables require thicker, stiffer bundles of shielded twisted-pair wiring. Single-link cables with omitted pins are lighter, thinner, and easier to route behind desks and displays.

Compatibility with DVI Ports

Graphics cards and monitors almost universally feature full 24-hole (plus flat ground bar) dual-link DVI female receptacles. Because a single-link DVI-D cable merely omits the unused pins rather than adding obstacles, it fits cleanly into these dual-link sockets. The socket simply receives no connection on the secondary data channels, prompting the hardware to automatically operate in single-link mode.
