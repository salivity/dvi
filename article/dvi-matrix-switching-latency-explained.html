DVI Matrix Switching Latency Explained

Digital matrix switching of DVI feeds generally introduces minimal latency, ranging from virtually zero nanoseconds in direct routing systems to one or two frames (16 to 33 milliseconds) in systems that utilize active scaling or frame synchronization. The exact delay depends entirely on whether the switch acts as an unscaled physical crosspoint or actively decodes, buffers, and processes the video signal to ensure seamless transitions.

Direct Crosspoint Switching (Near-Zero Latency)

In a pure unscaled digital matrix switcher, DVI signals—transmitted via Transition-Minimized Differential Signaling (TMDS)—are routed directly via an internal hardware crosspoint IC.

  • Latency: Under 1 microsecond (practically zero).
  • Behavior: The switch does not buffer or alter the pixel clock, meaning the signal arrives at the display with no perceptible delay.
  • Drawback: Switching between sources with different refresh rates, resolutions, or clock phases often causes the receiving display to lose sync momentarily, resulting in a brief black screen (typically 1 to 3 seconds) while the display resynchronizes.

Scaled and Seamless Matrix Switching (1 to 2 Frames of Latency)

To eliminate the black screen associated with source changes, many modern digital matrix switchers incorporate internal scalers or frame synchronizers.

  • Latency: Typically 1 to 2 frames. At a standard 60 Hz refresh rate, this equates to roughly 16.67 ms to 33.33 ms of latency.
  • Behavior: The matrix switcher captures the incoming DVI feed into a frame buffer, scales or synchronizes the timing to a fixed output resolution, and continuously sends a stable signal to the display.
  • Impact: While 16 to 33 ms is acceptable for broadcast, presentations, and general monitoring, it may be noticeable in real-time interactive applications, such as high-precision simulation, competitive gaming, or direct mouse-and-keyboard operation (KVM).

Modular Chassis and Signal Conversion Overhead

When DVI matrix switchers use modular input/output boards converting signals to proprietary protocols, fiber optic lines, or HDBaseT:

  • Uncompressed Extenders: Conversion to uncompressed formats (like standard HDBaseT or direct optical transmission) adds negligible delay, generally staying under a few microseconds.
  • Compression/Streaming Modules: If the DVI signal is encoded (e.g., via H.264, H.265, or JPEG 2000) for distribution over an IP-based matrix infrastructure, processing and packetization introduce latency ranging from 30 ms up to 250 ms depending on the compression profile used.

Switching Speed vs. Signal Latency

It is critical to distinguish between switching speed (the time it takes the hardware to swap inputs after a command is sent) and signal latency (the transport delay from input to output once the connection is established). HDCP handshaking and EDID processing can delay the initial source swap, but they do not add continuous latency to the live DVI video feed once the stream is established.
