Why Clock Jitter Causes DVI PLL Loss of Sync

This article examines why clock jitter in a Digital Visual Interface (DVI) cable disrupts the receiver's Phase-Locked Loop (PLL). When electrical degradation within the cable introduces excessive phase variations onto the transmission clock line, the receiver's internal PLL cannot accurately track the shifts. This breakdown in frequency and phase tracking shrinks the receiver's timing margins, eventually forcing the loop out of lock and causing visual artifacts, dropped frames, or a complete loss of the video signal.

The Role of the PLL in DVI Architecture

DVI relies on Transition Minimized Differential Signaling (TMDS) to transmit digital video data. Unlike interfaces that embed the clock within the data stream, standard DVI dedicates a specific twisted pair to carry an external reference clock. This clock runs at the pixel rate (1x), while the three accompanying data channels transmit pixel components at ten times the clock rate (10x).

To read incoming data correctly, the DVI receiver utilizes a Phase-Locked Loop (PLL). The PLL locks onto the incoming 1x clock signal, multiplies its frequency by a factor of 10, and generates an internal sampling clock. This high-speed internal clock determines the exact moment the receiver samples each bit within the incoming serial data streams.

How Cable Jitter Strains the PLL

As the clock signal travels along a DVI cable, it encounters physical imperfections, including conductor resistance, dielectric loss, skin effect, and inadequate shielding. These factors induce both deterministic jitter (such as inter-symbol interference and crosstalk from adjacent data pairs) and random jitter (thermal noise). This jitter manifests as temporal displacement, meaning the clock's voltage transitions occur earlier or later than their ideal points in time.

The receiver's PLL is designed with a specific loop bandwidth, which defines its ability to track phase variations:

  • Low-Frequency Jitter (Wander): If the phase variations occur at a frequency lower than the PLL loop bandwidth, the PLL adjusts its Voltage-Controlled Oscillator (VCO) to follow the jitter. The internal sampling clock shifts alongside the reference clock.
  • High-Frequency Jitter: If the cable introduces high-frequency jitter that exceeds the loop bandwidth, the PLL's Phase Frequency Detector (PFD) and loop filter cannot react quickly enough to track the rapid shifts.

The Threshold of Desynchronization

When high-frequency jitter or severe cycle-to-cycle phase deviations occur, the Phase Frequency Detector receives clock edges outside of its expected phase comparison window. If the timing discrepancy surpasses the phase detector's dynamic operating range, the error voltage delivered to the VCO fluctuates wildly.

Once the cumulative jitter exceeds the lock range (or tracking range) of the PLL, two primary failure modes occur:

  1. Phase Margin Collapse: Even if the PLL remains nominally locked to the fundamental frequency, the jitter creates a phase offset between the multiplied sampling clock and the data channels. This closes the "data eye," meaning the receiver samples the data lines during transition states rather than during stable intervals, leading to pixel sparkling and digital noise.
  2. Total Loop Unlock: When the phase error becomes too erratic or large for the charge pump and loop filter to correct, the feedback loop breaks down entirely. The VCO frequency drifts away from the target frequency, forcing the PLL out of lock.

When the PLL loses lock, the receiver cannot reconstruct the serial data into coherent pixel words. The display controller detects the absence of a stable clock, forcing the receiver to reset the TMDS link. The visible result is a momentary screen blackout or a persistent "No Signal" state until the jitter subsides and the PLL successfully re-acquires synchronization.
