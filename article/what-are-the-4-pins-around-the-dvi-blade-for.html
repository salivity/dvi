What Are the 4 Pins Around the DVI Blade For?

The four analog pins surrounding the flat ground blade on a DVI-I connector transmit legacy RGB analog video signals alongside a dedicated ground. While the primary rectangular grid of pins handles digital data, these four quadrant pins allow a DVI-I port to carry full analog VGA signals simultaneously, enabling passive adapters to connect modern DVI-I sources to older VGA monitors.

The Dual-Link Architecture of DVI

The Digital Visual Interface standard was engineered during a transitional era in display technology, bridging the gap between legacy cathode-ray tube (CRT) monitors and newer flat-panel digital screens. To avoid forcing users to purchase multiple graphics cards, the specification introduced three distinct connector configurations: DVI-D for digital-only video, DVI-A for rare analog-only setups, and DVI-I for integrated digital and analog video.

The main 24-pin block provides transition-minimized differential signaling (TMDS) channels for purely digital audio and video transmission. However, graphics cards equipped with DVI-I also feature an extended cross-shaped analog section on the side. This section contains a broad horizontal blade flanked above and below by four discrete analog pins, providing backward compatibility without active signal conversion.

Pinout and Functional Breakdown

The four separate pins surrounding the central blade form a dedicated analog pathway. Each pin carries a specific component of an analog video stream:

  • C1 (Top Right): Analog Red Signal. Carries the analog red color channel voltage to the display.
  • C2 (Bottom Right): Analog Green Signal. Carries the analog green color channel voltage, which can also carry sync-on-green pulses in select systems.
  • C3 (Top Left): Analog Blue Signal. Carries the analog blue color channel voltage.
  • C4 (Bottom Left): Analog Horizontal Sync. Carries the horizontal synchronization pulse that tells CRT and early LCD monitors when to scan across the display lines.

The flat bar in the center, designated as pin C5, acts as the analog ground return line for these color signals. Vertical synchronization and display data channel lines (DDC) are routed through specific shared pins inside the primary digital grid, allowing the computer to identify display parameters via EDID even when running over analog mode.

Why These Pins Matter for Adapters

Because these four pins output standard 0.7V peak-to-peak RGB video alongside dedicated sync lines, a DVI-I to VGA adapter does not require any digital-to-analog converter chips or external power. The adapter simply routes the C1 through C4 pins, along with the C5 ground and sync lines, straight into the corresponding pins of a standard 15-pin DE-15 (VGA) connector.

Attempting to plug a passive DVI-to-VGA adapter into a DVI-D port fails physically because digital-only ports omit the four analog pin holes. Today, modern display standards like DisplayPort and HDMI have fully replaced analog video pathways, but the quadrant pins on DVI-I remain a prominent example of legacy backward-compatibility engineering in personal computing.
