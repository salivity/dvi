Is Stripping HDCP from DVI Legal for Archival?

Stripping High-bandwidth Digital Content Protection (HDCP) signals from a DVI connection for archival purposes is generally illegal under modern digital copyright laws, most notably the United States’ Digital Millennium Copyright Act (DMCA). Although copyright law frequently grants individuals the right to create backup copies or utilize media under fair use doctrines, anti-circumvention statutes treat the act of bypassing digital rights management (DRM) as an independent legal violation. Consequently, bypassing HDCP encryption to preserve or record protected media is unlawful in most jurisdictions, regardless of whether your ultimate intent is benign personal archiving.

Understanding HDCP on DVI Connections

HDCP is an encryption standard developed by Intel to prevent unauthorized duplication of digital audio and video content as it travels across physical interfaces, such as DVI, HDMI, and DisplayPort. When a playback device (such as a computer or Blu-ray player) connects to a display or capture card via DVI, the devices initiate a cryptographic handshake. If the handshake succeeds, the encrypted video stream is transmitted; if it fails or the receiving device lacks HDCP compliance, the signal is blocked or downgraded to lower resolutions.

Stripping HDCP involves placing an intermediary device—such as a specialized splitter, converter, or capture box—between the transmitter and receiver to bypass the cryptographic handshake and output an unencrypted digital stream.

The Anti-Circumvention Rule (DMCA Section 1201)

The primary barrier to legally stripping HDCP in the United States is Section 1201 of the DMCA. Under this statute, it is unlawful to circumvent any technological measure that effectively controls access to a copyrighted work.

The law makes a strict distinction between traditional copyright infringement and the act of circumvention itself:

  • Access Control Violation: Bypassing an encryption protocol like HDCP is classified as defeating an access control mechanism. The DMCA prohibits this act outright, irrespective of how the resulting decrypted data is handled.
  • Separation from Fair Use: Courts have consistently ruled that fair use does not serve as a valid defense against the act of circumvention. While you might have a fair use right to excerpt, view, or archive certain media, you do not have the legal right to break the technological lock protecting that media to exercise those rights.

Every three years, the U.S. Copyright Office and the Library of Congress review exemptions to Section 1201. While exemptions have been granted for specific preservation tasks—such as computer programs, video games requiring abandoned authentication servers, or short film clips for educational use—no broad exemption permits consumers to bypass HDCP to archive commercial video streams.

Hardware and Trafficking Provisions

The DMCA does not just prohibit the individual user from running or activating circumvention measures; it also outlaws the manufacture, import, sale, or distribution of devices designed primarily to circumvent access controls.

Hardware devices that strip HDCP metadata from DVI or HDMI links fall directly under these anti-trafficking provisions (Sections 1201(a)(2) and 1201(b)). Major rights holders and the Digital Content Protection LLC (the entity licensing HDCP) regularly pursue legal action against companies that sell "HDCP strippers," leading to injunctions, device seizures, and severe statutory damages.

International Perspectives

The legal prohibition against stripping HDCP is not exclusive to the United States. Most developed nations have adopted similar frameworks to comply with the 1996 World Intellectual Property Organization (WIPO) Copyright Treaty:

  • European Union: The EU Copyright Directive (Directive 2001/29/EC) mandates legal protection against the circumvention of effective technological protection measures (TPMs). Member states maintain laws that penalize bypassing encryption, often limiting exceptions to institutional archives and libraries rather than individuals.
  • United Kingdom, Canada, and Australia: These jurisdictions enforce similar TPM rules that penalize the circumvention of digital locks, typically preventing consumer-level hardware stripping for archival reasons.

Conclusion

Even if your intention is solely to archive legally acquired content for historical preservation or personal backup, stripping HDCP signals from a DVI link violates anti-circumvention laws. Because the law treats the removal of encryption as a standalone infraction independent of copyright infringement, the act remains illegal without an explicit statutory exemption.
