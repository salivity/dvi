Why DVI is Popular in Retrocomputing and FPGA Emulation

Digital Visual Interface (DVI) remains an enduring standard in the retrocomputing and field-programmable gate array (FPGA) development communities. Despite being largely replaced by DisplayPort and HDMI in modern consumer electronics, DVI offers a unique combination of native analog and digital support, pin-level compatibility with HDMI, zero royalty restrictions, and lower hardware overhead. This article explains the technical and practical advantages that make DVI a preferred video interface for vintage PC enthusiasts and hardware emulation platforms like the MiSTer FPGA.

Native Support for Both Digital and Analog Signals

The primary strength of the full DVI specification (specifically DVI-I) is its ability to route both digital and analog signals through a single port. The pinout includes dedicated lines for Transition-Minimized Differential Signaling (TMDS) digital data alongside standard RGB analog pins with horizontal and vertical sync.

In retrocomputing and emulation setups, this allows users to connect either a modern flat-panel monitor via a digital signal or a classic CRT monitor via an analog VGA signal using a simple, passive adapter. Because no active signal conversion or digital-to-analog processing chip is strictly required inside an external adapter, video latency is eliminated, and native resolutions are preserved.

Hardware and Protocol Compatibility with HDMI

The digital component of DVI (DVI-D) uses the exact same single-link TMDS signaling protocol as basic HDMI video. Electrically, a digital DVI output can be wired directly to an HDMI display using an inexpensive passive cable.

For FPGA projects, this means developers can target modern HDMI-capable displays without having to adhere to complicated physical HDMI jack form factors or complex proprietary board layouts. The video output remains pixel-perfect and introduces zero processing latency when routed to modern screens.

Absence of Licensing and Royalty Fees

HDMI is a proprietary standard governed by HDMI Licensing Administrator, Inc., requiring manufacturers and hardware designers to pay annual adopter fees and per-unit royalties. For open-source hardware communities, boutique FPGA console developers, and open-hardware retro computing groups, these licensing costs are prohibitive.

DVI, developed by the Digital Display Working Group (DDWG), carries no such ongoing licensing or compliance requirements. Hardware designers can integrate DVI ports into open-source PCB designs, publish schematics publicly, and sell low-volume production runs without legal hurdles or expensive compliance testing.

Reduced FPGA Logic Utilization

Generating an authentic digital video stream within an FPGA requires allocating limited internal logic units (Look-Up Tables, or LUTs). Implementing a full HDMI specification requires significant logic overhead to manage audio data packets, auxiliary metadata, InfoFrames, and HDCP encryption blocks.

By contrast, generating a raw DVI signal requires only the core TMDS serialization logic for the red, green, blue, and clock channels. This drastically reduces gate utilization, freeing up critical FPGA fabric to emulate the actual computer architectures, sound chips, and retro console processors accurately.

Flexibility with Non-Standard Retro Video Timing

Vintage computers and game consoles rarely output standardized video signals. Systems frequently relied on unusual resolutions (such as 256x224 or 320x240) and atypical refresh rates (such as 59.18 Hz or 50.08 Hz).

Modern digital transceivers built directly into specialized HDMI transmitter chips often reject these non-standard timings or attempt to force them into rigid modern broadcast standards, resulting in frame judder or black screens. DVI pipelines, operating at a lower, unmanaged hardware layer, allow developers to stream raw, unconstrained pixel clocks directly from the FPGA core to the display.
