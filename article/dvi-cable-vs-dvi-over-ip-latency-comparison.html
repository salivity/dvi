DVI Cable vs DVI-over-IP: Latency Comparison

Native DVI copper cables deliver virtually instantaneous signal transmission with latency measured in nanoseconds, while DVI-over-IP extenders introduce measurable delay ranging from sub-millisecond figures to over 100 milliseconds. This difference stems from how each technology handles video data: native copper utilizes direct electrical conductivity over short distances, whereas DVI-over-IP requires signal conversion, compression, network packetization, and decoding to send data across an Ethernet network.

Latency in Native DVI Copper Cables

A native DVI copper cable transmits digital red, green, blue, and clock signals via Transition-Minimized Differential Signaling (TMDS). Because this process involves no conversion, compression, or processing buffers, transmission speed is dictated entirely by the propagation speed of electricity through copper.

Electricity travels through copper cable at roughly two-thirds the speed of light, yielding a propagation delay of approximately 5 nanoseconds per meter. Over a standard 5-meter (16-foot) DVI cable, the total transmission delay is roughly 25 nanoseconds. From a human perspective and for any standard computing hardware, native DVI operates at true "zero latency," making it the ideal standard for real-time interactivity, precision mouse tracking, competitive gaming, and local desktop use. However, native copper cables are limited to short runs, usually degrading in signal quality past 5 to 10 meters without active amplification.

Latency in DVI-over-IP Extenders

DVI-over-IP systems bypass the distance limits of copper by converting video frames into network packets, enabling distribution over local area networks (LANs) across hundreds of meters or across campus networks. This flexibility introduces several stages of processing delay:

  • Capture and Compression: The transmitter hardware captures the incoming TMDS stream and, depending on the bandwidth architecture, compresses the video. Lightweight compression schemes (such as JPEG-XS or proprietary wavelet codecs) take less than a millisecond, while high-efficiency codecs (such as H.264 or H.265) require full-frame buffers, adding anywhere from 16 milliseconds (one frame at 60 Hz) to over 60 milliseconds.
  • Packetization and Switching: The encoded stream is packaged into standard IP packets (often UDP/RTP) and transmitted over standard Ethernet switches. Network switches, queuing mechanisms, and network jitter buffers add an additional 1 to 5 milliseconds.
  • Depacketization and Decoding: The receiver unpacks the network stream, buffers the video data to account for network jitter, decodes the frames back into a native video signal, and outputs it to the display.

Because of these variables, latency across DVI-over-IP systems falls into two distinct categories:

  1. Ultra-Low Latency / Uncompressed (typically 10GbE): Systems using high-bandwidth 10-Gigabit networks often transmit uncompressed video or use minimal line-based compression. These models deliver latency between 0.1 milliseconds and 5 milliseconds (less than one-third of a frame), which is visually imperceptible to most users for standard desktop and interactive tasks.
  2. Standard Network / Compressed (typically 1GbE): Systems engineered to fit existing 1-Gigabit infrastructure rely on heavier compression algorithms. Total end-to-end latency for these systems commonly ranges from 30 to 150 milliseconds. At this level, users experience noticeable mouse lag, visual delay during interactive input, and lip-sync synchronization issues if audio is not carefully aligned.

Summary Comparison

Native DVI copper provides true zero-latency performance (nanoseconds) but cannot span long physical distances without signal degradation. DVI-over-IP enables virtually unlimited transmission range and routing flexibility over network infrastructure, but it incurs a latency penalty ranging from a fraction of a millisecond on high-end 10GbE systems to several frames on bandwidth-constrained 1GbE networks.
