How HDCP Prevents Active DVI Signal Conversion

High-bandwidth Digital Content Protection (HDCP) prevents the unauthorized active conversion of encrypted DVI signals by combining continuous cryptographic encryption, strict hardware authentication, and enforceable license restrictions. When a video source transmits an encrypted DVI stream, any active converter must act as an authorized, fully compliant HDCP receiver before it can process the signal. If an active converter attempts to alter, digitize, or transform the encrypted stream into an unprotected format—such as standard analog VGA or an unencrypted digital stream—without adhering to cryptographic and licensing rules, the hardware handshake fails, revoking decryption access and blocking content delivery entirely.

The Role of Active Converters in DVI Transmissions

Active conversion involves receiving an incoming video signal, decoding its data, and actively re-encoding or translating it into another standard, such as converting digital DVI to analog VGA or DisplayPort. Because DVI uses Transition-Minimized Differential Signaling (TMDS) to transmit uncompressed digital video, an unprotected signal could easily be duplicated without quality loss. HDCP acts as a security layer wrapped around this TMDS transmission, ensuring that active processing can only happen within closed, trusted cryptographic boundaries.

Authentication and Key Exchange (AKE)

Before any video data is transmitted, the source device (such as a media player or computer) and the active converter must execute an Authentication and Key Exchange (AKE).

  1. Exchange of Keys: The source and the active converter exchange identification vectors known as Key Selection Vectors (KSVs).
  2. Secret Computation: Both devices compute a shared secret key using their internal, secret hardware keys alongside the partner's KSV.
  3. Verification: The source ensures the converter possesses a legitimate cryptographic identity granted by Digital Content Protection LLC (DCP), the governing body of HDCP.

If an active converter does not possess genuine, licensed cryptographic keys, the exchange immediately fails. Without a successful handshake, the source simply refuses to transmit the unencrypted video stream or outputs an intentionally blank, static, or low-resolution warning screen.

Continuous Stream Encryption

Once authenticated, the DVI transmitter applies an HDCP cipher to encrypt the pixel data transmitted across the TMDS lines on a frame-by-frame basis. Active conversion requires deciphering these individual pixels to transform them into another signaling standard.

Because the encryption operates in real time and relies on synchronized pseudo-random number generators operating on both ends of the cable, an active converter cannot bypass the cipher via simple passive tapping or unauthorized signal modification. The converter must actively decrypt the stream using the shared cipher states calculated during authentication.

Licensing and Output Restrictions

HDCP compliance is enforced not only by mathematics but by strict hardware licensing rules. For a manufacturer to obtain valid HDCP keys to build an active converter, they must sign compliance agreements that legally dictate device capabilities:

  • No Unprotected Digital Outputs: An active converter cannot decrypt an HDCP-protected DVI input and transmit it over an unencrypted digital output.
  • Prohibition of High-Resolution Analog Conversion: Compliant converters are strictly forbidden from outputting full-resolution, unencrypted analog signals (such as converting protected 1080p DVI to standard RGB/VGA) without applying restrictions, such as downscaling or outright signal blanking.
  • Tamper Resistance: The physical hardware of the converter must be designed to resist reverse engineering or electrical probing that could expose raw, unencrypted digital signals inside the device housing before re-encoding takes place.

Revocation of Compromised Converters

To handle instances where devices are modified or keys are cloned to illegally strip HDCP during conversion, the system utilizes System Renewability Messages (SRMs). SRMs are distributed via newer media discs, operating system updates, or graphic drivers, containing lists of compromised KSVs.

When a source identifies that an attached active converter's KSV has been revoked, the source terminates the connection. This mechanism guarantees that unauthorized active converters designed to strip DVI encryption are isolated and rendered unusable across modern playback hardware.
