Nvidia SPDIF Passthrough Cable for DVI Explained

Before graphics cards had integrated digital audio processors, Nvidia used an internal two-pin SPDIF passthrough cable to transmit audio alongside video over DVI and HDMI connections. Connecting directly between the motherboard or sound card and the graphics card, this cable allowed digital stereo or compressed surround sound to be injected into the video stream. This guide explains why the cable existed, how it functioned, and why it eventually became obsolete.

The Technical Problem

During the mid-to-late 2000s—prominently during the era of the GeForce 8, 9, and early 200 series—televisions with HDMI inputs became the standard display for home theater PCs and gaming setups. While HDMI carried both high-definition video and audio on a single cable, PC graphics cards typically featured dual-link DVI ports.

Although DVI and HDMI share the exact same digital video signaling (TMDS), standard DVI did not natively support audio transmission. Furthermore, older Nvidia graphics processing units (GPUs) lacked an integrated high-definition audio controller. Competing ATI/AMD Radeon cards (starting with the Radeon HD 2000 series) resolved this by embedding an independent audio processor directly onto the GPU silicon, but Nvidia opted for a hardware workaround to avoid redesigning their GPU architecture.

How the SPDIF Passthrough Cable Worked

Nvidia's solution was a simple, internal two-wire cable. One wire carried the digital S/PDIF (Sony/Philips Digital Interface) audio signal, and the other served as the electrical ground.

To set it up, users had to:

  1. Locate the internal S/PDIF digital audio output pins on their motherboard or dedicated sound card.
  2. Connect one end of the two-pin lead to the motherboard’s SPDIF_OUT and GND headers.
  3. Plug the other end into a small white or black two-pin header located near the top edge or power connectors of the Nvidia graphics card.

Once connected, the graphics card did not process or decode the audio. Instead, it acted as a multiplexer. The GPU took the incoming digital audio stream and interleaved the audio packets into the blanking intervals of the DVI or HDMI video signal. When a user attached a proprietary DVI-to-HDMI adapter or plugged directly into an onboard HDMI port, the connected television or AV receiver received both digital picture and sound through a single HDMI cable.

Limitations of the Setup

While functional, this approach had several constraints:

  • Format Limitations: Because S/PDIF has limited bandwidth, it could only transmit uncompressed 2-channel stereo PCM or compressed bitstream formats such as Dolby Digital and DTS 5.1. It could not carry uncompressed multi-channel LPCM or high-bitrate formats like Dolby TrueHD or DTS-HD Master Audio.
  • Complex Installation: Users often had to verify motherboard pinouts manually, as plugging the ground and data pins backward could prevent audio delivery.
  • Operating System Configuration: The operating system had to be manually configured to use the motherboard's S/PDIF digital output as the default playback device rather than regular analog speaker outputs.

Obsolescence

The need for the external SPDIF jumper cable ended as Nvidia redesigned its hardware. Entry-level cards like the GeForce GT 210 and GT 220 were among the first to feature built-in HD audio controllers. By the launch of the GeForce 400 series (Fermi architecture) in 2010, native internal audio controllers became standard across all Nvidia graphics cards, enabling plug-and-play audio over native HDMI and DisplayPort interfaces without extra internal wiring.
