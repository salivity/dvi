Can DVI-I Output Analog and Digital at the Same Time?

A DVI-I (Digital Video Interface - Integrated) port cannot output both independent analog and digital video signals simultaneously to drive two separate displays. Although the physical DVI-I connector contains separate pins dedicated to digital (TMDS) and analog (VGA) signals, standard consumer graphics cards and motherboards are not engineered to power both signal pathways on a single port at the same time. This article explains the technical limitations of DVI-I ports, why passive splitters will not provide dual independent desktops, and the appropriate alternatives for multi-monitor setups.

Understanding the DVI-I Connector

DVI-I is a hybrid standard designed for backward compatibility during the transition from cathode-ray tube (CRT) monitors to digital flat panels. The connector houses two distinct sets of pins:

  • Digital Pins: The 24-pin grid carries digital Transition-Minimized Differential Signaling (TMDS), which transmits digital data directly to digital monitors (DVI-D or HDMI via an adapter).
  • Analog Pins: The four pins surrounding the flat blade on the side carry analog RGB signals and synchronization pulses, equivalent to a standard VGA connection.

While both sets of pins exist side-by-side in the same physical plug, their purpose is versatility, not dual-stream capability.

Why Simultaneous Output Does Not Work

The limitation lies inside the graphics processing unit (GPU), not the cable. A standard GPU links each physical port to a single internal display pipeline (display engine). When you boot a system or connect a screen, the graphics card uses the Display Data Channel (DDC) to read the monitor’s Extended Display Identification Data (EDID).

Through this handshake, the GPU determines whether the attached monitor requires an analog or digital signal:

  • If an analog display is detected, the GPU routes video data through a Random Access Memory Digital-to-Analog Converter (RAMDAC) to the analog pins.
  • If a digital display is detected, the GPU routes data to the TMDS transmitter.

Because only one display pipeline is assigned to the DVI-I port, the hardware cannot drive both the RAMDAC and the TMDS transmitter simultaneously for that port. It must choose one signal path.

What Happens with DVI-I Splitter Cables?

Passive "DVI-I to DVI-D and VGA" splitter cables exist on the market, but their functionality is widely misunderstood. These cables merely separate the analog pins to a VGA connector and the digital pins to a DVI-D connector.

If you connect two monitors to a single DVI-I port using one of these splitters:

  1. EDID Conflicts: The graphics card will typically read the EDID handshake of only one display (often the digital one by default).
  2. One Active Display: Only one monitor will receive an active signal. The secondary monitor will remain black or display a "No Signal" prompt.
  3. No Extended Desktop: Even in the rare event of hardware quirkiness, the GPU cannot render two distinct framebuffers through one port. You cannot achieve an extended desktop layout.

DVI-I vs. DMS-59

A common point of confusion is the DMS-59 port, an interface frequently found on low-profile, business-class graphics cards. While DMS-59 looks somewhat similar to a DVI port, it is explicitly designed to carry two independent video channels—either two digital signals, two analog signals, or one of each—using a dedicated "Y-splitter" cable. Standard DVI-I lacks this internal wiring and will not function in this manner.

How to Achieve Dual Displays

To run two independent displays from a computer relying on legacy ports, consider the following alternatives:

  • Use Multiple Native GPU Ports: The most reliable method is to connect one monitor to the DVI-I port and the second monitor to another available output on your graphics card or motherboard (such as a secondary DVI, HDMI, or DisplayPort).
  • DisplayPort Multi-Stream Transport (MST): If your system has a modern DisplayPort output, you can daisy-chain monitors or use an MST hub to drive multiple distinct displays from a single port.
  • USB Display Adapters: If no other video ports are available on your system, an external USB-to-VGA or USB-to-HDMI adapter powered by DisplayLink technology can provide a secondary video output.
