Routing CEC Signals Over Non-Standard DVI Cables

While the official Digital Visual Interface (DVI) specification does not define or support Consumer Electronics Control (CEC), it is technically possible to route a CEC signal over a non-standard or custom-wired DVI cable. Because CEC is a low-speed, single-wire serial bus, any physically continuous copper pathway with proper grounding can carry the signal between compatible endpoints. This article details the electrical feasibility, required pin configurations, hardware limitations, and practical considerations of routing CEC through non-standard DVI cabling.

The Electrical Nature of CEC

Consumer Electronics Control is a bidirectional, single-wire communication bus designed to allow HDMI-connected devices to control one another using a single remote. It operates using an open-drain/collector topology at roughly 3.3 volts with low-frequency timing (sub-1 kHz). Because it operates at such low frequencies, CEC does not require specialized shielding or high-frequency impedance matching, making ordinary copper conductors fully capable of carrying the signal without significant attenuation over typical cable runs.

Standard vs. Non-Standard DVI Pinouts

In standard DVI-to-HDMI adapters and cables, pin 13 of the HDMI connector (the designated CEC line) is left disconnected, floating, or tied to ground. The DVI specification simply does not allocate a pin for consumer control commands, focusing entirely on video transmission (via TMDS pairs) and display discovery (via DDC lines).

To route CEC over a non-standard DVI cable, a customized pin assignment is required:

  • Single-Link Implementations: A single-link DVI connection leaves several pins unused, specifically the pins designated for TMDS Link 2 (pins 4, 5, 12, 13, 20, and 21). Any of these unassigned conductors can be repurposed to carry the HDMI pin 13 CEC signal.
  • DVI-I Connectors: If using a DVI-Integrated (DVI-I) connector for a purely digital display, the analog pins (C1 through C4) are unutilized and can readily serve as a CEC transmission path.
  • Ground Reference: CEC requires a common ground reference to function reliably. As long as the DVI shield or standard digital ground pins (such as pins 3, 11, 19, or 22) are connected on both ends, the CEC circuit will complete normally.

Hardware and Controller Constraints

Routing the physical electrical signal through the cable is only half the requirement. Hardware devices on both ends of the cable must possess the capability to process the signal:

  1. Native DVI Devices Lack Controllers: Standard graphics cards, monitors, and projectors with native DVI ports do not contain the CEC microcontrollers or internal firmware needed to transmit or receive CEC commands. Simply connecting a non-standard cable to a standard DVI port will not enable CEC functionality on that device.
  2. HDMI-to-DVI Pass-Through Scenarios: The primary use case for a non-standard DVI cable carrying CEC is routing signals between two HDMI-enabled devices through an infrastructure path that relies on DVI connectors (such as an existing wall plate or custom patch bay). In this scenario, HDMI pin 13 is mapped to a chosen DVI pin on one end and broken out back to HDMI pin 13 on the other. Because both active endpoints are HDMI hardware, CEC communication functions as intended.

Summary

A non-standard DVI cable can easily carry a Consumer Electronics Control signal from a purely physical and electrical perspective. However, because native DVI source and display hardware lack the controllers and firmware necessary to parse CEC, this setup is only practical when using custom cabling to bridge two HDMI devices across a DVI-based physical connection.
